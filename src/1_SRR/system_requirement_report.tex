\section{System Concept and Architecture}

The Texas A\&M Space Platform Integrating Research and Innovative Technology (TAMU-SPIRIT) is an externally mounted, multi-payload flight facility deployed on the International Space Station (ISS), developed in partnership with Aegis Aerospace \cite{tamuSpiritWeb_2025,aggie-space-lab-tamu-spirit}. The facility uses a modular, carrier-based architecture capable of hosting up to twelve Science Carriers (SC) or Pallet Carriers (PC) simultaneously. Each carrier position provides standardized mechanical, electrical, and data interfaces for hosted flight experiments.

TAMU-SPIRIT operates in low Earth orbit (LEO) at approximately 400 km altitude, exposing payloads to atomic oxygen, radiation, hard vacuum, and thermal cycling from roughly $-120\,^{\circ}$C to $+120\,^{\circ}$C over each 90-minute orbital period. Deployment cycles are six to twelve months, after which carriers and experiment hardware are returned to Earth for post-flight analysis via Dragon downmass operations \cite{iss-national-lab-multi-purpose,gunter-misse-ff,alpha-space-misse}.

At the system level, each carrier is assigned a designated mounting location with predefined interface boundaries. SC hardware is constrained by smaller envelope and mass limits, while PC hardware supports larger form factors and higher mass. Carrier slots support 28 VDC power distribution and bounded command/data transfer through TAMU-SPIRIT operations pathways. Carrier orientation options (Ram, Wake, Nadir, and Zenith) are selected based on experiment objectives and environmental exposure intent.

For the current SRR cycle, ten candidate flight experiments are under development: Thermal Dust Sensor Exposure (TDS), Micro-Gravity Vortex Separator (MGVS), Cryogenic Preservation of Mammalian Organs (CPMO), Autonomous Classification and Tracking of Plumes (ACT-P), Spaceflight Resilience of Food Fermentation Fungi (SRFFF), Freeze-Dried Food Analogs (FDFA), Diels--Alder Self-Healing Exposure (DASH), Event-Based Space Telescope (EBST), AM Exposure, and Phage Biofilms. The system architecture remains centered on standard TAMU-SPIRIT carrier integration while allowing experiment-specific sensing, containment, and autonomy strategies.

\subsection{1. Thermal Dust Sensor Exposure (TDS)}

The Thermal Dust Sensor Exposure experiment (TDS) is an active flight experiment that passed initial downselection. The mission is to modify a pre-designed thermal dust sensor for TAMU-SPIRIT compatibility and quantify sensor degradation under long-duration space exposure. Primary goals are to estimate emissivity evolution in orbit (TDS-G1) and compare on-orbit behavior against control datasets to quantify radiative degradation (TDS-G2) \cite{kurwitz-lsep-2026}.

\subsection{2. Micro-Gravity Vortex Separator (MGVS)}

The Micro-Gravity Vortex Separator (MGVS) concept aims to demonstrate gravity-independent Rankine-cycle-enabling behavior through vortex phase separation. Goals include fabricating and characterizing key condenser/boiler components (MGVS-G1) and refining experimentally derived models with flight-linked evidence (MGVS-G2). This concept was later dropped for this cycle due to complexity and resource burden, but requirements are preserved for traceability.

\subsection{3. Cryogenic Preservation of Mammalian Organs (CPMO)}

The objective of this experiment is to design a space-compliant cryogenic system that preserves vitrified mammalian organs for at least six months before return for transplantation studies. Key goals are to maintain structural and isochoric containment autonomy under TAMU-SPIRIT constraints (CPMO-G1) and preserve specimen vitrification and separation throughout mission operations (CPMO-G2).

The objective of these analyses is to determine viability of the remaining flight experiments and support a structured downselect to a set of constructable and validatable experiments for AERO 402 execution. Analysis outputs will convert high-level requirements into bounded, verifiable requirements by resolving open technical risks such as thermal margins, EMI compliance, structural loading, and contamination control.

To support consistent decision-making, each experiment will be evaluated against common gates:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Feasibility:} Implementable within expected TAMU-SPIRIT allocations and packaging constraints with no exceptional subsystem accommodation.
    \item \textbf{Safety/Compliance:} Meets ISS external payload expectations, including containment, contamination control, structural margins, and EMI compatibility.
    \item \textbf{Thermal/Power:} Maintains components within allowable operating and survival limits without exceeding allocated power.
    \item \textbf{Operations:} Can be commanded/monitored with available concepts while producing meaningful scientific results.
    \item \textbf{Verification:} Can be tested/analyzed within AERO 402 schedule and available resources with traceable evidence.
\end{itemize}

If an experiment fails a gate due to schedule or technical limitations, disposition paths are: (1) drop if safety/compliance or fundamental physical feasibility is not met, (2) handoff to a future AERO 401 cycle if safety/compliance passes but operational maturity is insufficient, or (3) handoff/continuation through AERO 402 for validation closure when operations are viable but verification remains incomplete.
